Organic vs Conventional: What the Evidence Actually Says

OLBy OrgLab Research DeskLast reviewed {{TODAY}}9 min readEvidence-based

Key takeaways

  • The strongest and most consistent difference is pesticide residues: they turn up far less often, and at lower levels, in organic produce.
  • Organic crops tend to have more antioxidants and less cadmium. Differences in vitamins and minerals are small.
  • Organic milk and meat contain about 50% more omega-3 fatty acids on average.
  • Links to better health outcomes come mostly from observational studies, which can't prove cause and effect.

1. Pesticide residues: the clearest difference

A 2014 meta-analysis of 343 peer-reviewed studies in the British Journal of Nutrition (Barański et al.) found pesticide residues were about four times more common in conventional crops than in organic ones. The 2012 Stanford review in Annals of Internal Medicine (Smith-Spangler et al.) put the reduction in risk of detectable residues at about 30%, while noting that residues in both groups rarely exceeded legal limits.

Diet studies point the same way. In a 2019 intervention study (Hyland et al., Environmental Research), US families switched to an all-organic diet for just six days. Their levels of urinary pesticide metabolites fell by an average of about 60%.

Bottom line: if your goal is to cut pesticide exposure, eating organic works, and it works fast.

2. Nutrients and antioxidants

The 2014 Barański meta-analysis reported substantially higher concentrations of several antioxidant compound classes in organic crops (often in the 18–69% range), which it attributed partly to plants' own defense responses when synthetic pesticides aren't used. Vitamin and mineral differences are generally small and inconsistent. The Stanford review found no strong evidence that organic foods are significantly more nutritious overall.

3. Cadmium and heavy metals

The same 2014 analysis found cadmium, a toxic heavy metal, was on average around 48% lower in organic crops. That's probably linked to not using certain mineral phosphate fertilizers. Organic status does not protect against soil-borne arsenic or lead, though. Rice and some leafy greens can carry these metals whatever the farming method. See our lab reports.

4. Milk and meat

Two 2016 meta-analyses (Średnicka-Tober et al., British Journal of Nutrition) found organic milk and meat contained about 50% more omega-3 fatty acids. The main reason is more grazing and grass-based feed.

5. Health outcomes

A large French cohort study (Baudry et al., JAMA Internal Medicine, 2018; ~69,000 adults) found the highest organic consumers had about a 25% lower overall cancer risk than the lowest. That's an association, not proof: people who eat organic tend to differ in many other ways too. Good long-term randomized trials don't exist.
