Pesticide Residues: How to Prioritize Your Organic Buys

OLBy OrgLab Research DeskLast reviewed {{TODAY}}8 min read

How residues are measured

The USDA Pesticide Data Program (PDP) tests thousands of food samples every year, and the samples are washed or peeled the way a consumer would prepare them. In recent annual reports, more than 99% of samples had residues below the tolerances set by the EPA, and a substantial share had no detectable residues at all.

“Below tolerance” doesn't mean zero

Tolerances are legal limits for each pesticide on each crop. They aren't health thresholds for combined exposure. Many samples carry several different residues at once, and researchers still debate the combined effects of low-dose mixtures. So residue-conscious shoppers look at how often residues appear and how many show up per sample, not only whether a limit was exceeded.

The 3-tier strategy

  1. Buy organic: high-residue, thin-skinned produce such as strawberries, spinach, kale, grapes, peaches and apples.
  2. Buy organic if the budget allows: moderate items such as celery, tomatoes, cucumbers and citrus (if you use the zest).
  3. Buy conventional without worry: thick-skinned, low-residue items such as avocados, pineapple, onions, mango and bananas.

Does washing work?

Rinsing under running water removes some surface residue. Research has shown baking-soda soaks remove more of certain surface pesticides. Neither removes systemic pesticides absorbed into the plant. Peeling helps but costs you fibre and nutrients.
