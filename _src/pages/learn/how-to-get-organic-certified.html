How to Get Organic Certified: 10 Steps, Costs & Timeline

OLBy OrgLab Research DeskLast reviewed {{TODAY}}12 min read

At a glance

  • Who needs it: any US operation selling more than $5,000 a year of products labeled organic. Since the Strengthening Organic Enforcement (SOE) rule took effect in March 2024, that also includes many brokers, traders and importers.
  • Timeline: about 3–6 months once land is eligible, and land must be free of prohibited substances for 36 months before harvest.
  • Year-one cost: roughly $1,000–$3,000 for small operations, much more for large brands, before cost-share.

Who needs organic certification?

In the United States, the USDA National Organic Program (NOP) regulates the word “organic” on agricultural products. Farms, ranches, processors and handlers that sell, label or represent products as organic must be certified by a USDA-accredited certifying agent. The main exception is operations with gross organic sales of $5,000 or less. They must still follow the rules, but they can't use the USDA seal or sell their products as ingredients in someone else's certified organic product.

The SOE rule (effective March 19, 2024) closed loopholes in the supply chain. More intermediaries now need certification, imports need NOP Import Certificates, and certifiers must run more unannounced inspections and supply-chain traceability checks.

The 10 steps

  1. Confirm eligibility. Document 36 months of land history without prohibited substances. Livestock have their own transition rules, such as the one-time transition for dairy herds.
  2. Choose your markets & standards. USDA NOP for the US. Canada (COR) and the EU have their own standards, and equivalency arrangements can reduce duplicate work.
  3. Pick an accredited certifier. Compare fees, inspector coverage in your area, turnaround time and experience with your scope. Check accreditation in the USDA Organic Integrity Database.
  4. Write your Organic System Plan (OSP). Describe your practices, inputs, seed sources, soil and pest management, buffer zones, contamination prevention, recordkeeping and maps.
  5. Submit the application. Include your OSP, maps, input lists, labels (for handlers) and the fees.
  6. Initial review. The certifier checks for completeness and compliance and may ask for more information.
  7. On-site inspection. An inspector checks fields, facilities, storage, records and traceability, often with a mass-balance or trace-back audit.
  8. Final review. The certifier assesses the inspection report. You may have to fix minor non-compliances.
  9. Certification. You receive your certificate and can use the USDA Organic seal. Get label approval before you print.
  10. Maintain it. Update your OSP every year, keep records for at least 5 years, get inspected annually, and claim cost-share.

The 5 most common delays

  • Incomplete land history or missing input documentation.
  • Using an input that hasn't been approved (always check with your certifier before applying anything new).
  • Weak audit trail: no lot numbers, harvest records or sales records.
  • No buffer zones or contamination-prevention plan next to conventional neighbours.
  • Labels printed before the certifier approved them.

Help during the transition

USDA's Organic Transition Initiative funds the Transition to Organic Partnership Program (TOPP), which pairs transitioning farmers with experienced mentors. NRCS conservation programs such as EQIP can also cost-share organic-compatible practices. Model your economics with the Transition ROI calculator.
